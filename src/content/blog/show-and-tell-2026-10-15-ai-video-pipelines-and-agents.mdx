Twelve builders took the floor at our October 15 Weekly Show & Tell in Kuala Lumpur. While topics ranged from WhatsApp personal assistants to Godot game development, the room converged on a clear theme: practical AI video production. Presenters shared real numbers on render times, API costs, script bottlenecks, and where human control still beats full automation.

## Automated Research, Scripting, and Storyboarding

Several teams focused on the upstream work that slows video production down before a single frame is rendered: gathering references, breaking down hooks, and organizing storyboards.

Shahbaaz Siamwala, founder of Leap Commerce, walked through an automated research pipeline built for brands selling on TikTok, Shopee, and Lazada. Instead of manually tagging videos, researchers drop raw TikTok and Instagram links into a Notion database called Ideas & Research Inbox. A daily scheduled job triggers Apify to scrape transcripts and engagement metrics (likes, comments, shares), and Claude enriches each record with cut counts, duration, narrative beats, and frame-by-frame visual hook notes, demonstrated on a clip from @eureka_snack_popcorn. Shahbaaz noted that initial link sourcing remains manual for now because off-the-shelf discovery tools run around $200 per month.

Lizzie Tan tackled the bottleneck that appears after editing is automated. Using Claude Code, she brought her Instagram video editing time down from three to five hours of manual work to roughly one hour of unattended processing, with a generated clip reaching 14,000 views. That shifted the main time sink to scripting, which still took 30 minutes to two hours. To address it, she built two web tools: one that transcribes creator videos (demonstrated on Jun Yuh) and breaks them into reusable narrative beat cards like open loops and surprising proof, and another that combines reference scripts, carousel points, and personal context into an 80 percent draft. Her main takeaway: passing raw transcripts straight to an LLM produces weak rewrites, and multi-source drafts still need a human pass to match your own voice.

Chloe Sin Yin showed how she produced over 20 short-form videos in two weeks without manual editing using Claude, Hyperframes, and Pexels. By placing raw footage and B-roll in a single local directory and feeding Claude upfront brand guidelines, she gets an initial storyboard and edit about 90 percent complete, leaving minor pacing and music tweaks. To avoid repeated 20 to 40 minute renders, she batches all review notes before triggering a render and previews short Hyperframes animation effects first. She also built a custom Storyboard web app with Claude to visualize scripts alongside reference cards, with a timeline view planned next, and screened a finished YES 5G AI 20 review video.

## End-to-End Video Pipelines and Timeline Control

A second group of demos looked at the architecture of video editors themselves, comparing agent-native pipelines against traditional timeline tools.

Andrew presented a local, agent-native video production system built from the ground up for AI agents rather than human editing UIs. Starting from a text prompt, the pipeline runs modular stages: story breakdown, visual plotting, character and environment design sheets, keyframing, multi-shot footage generation, multimodal music composition (analyzing both script text and generated frames), narration, timeline cuts, motion graphics, custom sound design, and automated color grading with self-review. The web dashboard is strictly read-only for monitoring, with all edits requested conversationally. Andrew observed that while generated video still shows AI artifacts, the sound design output is much stronger. He also demoed an automated speaker reel generator that takes a one-hour Show & Tell recording and cuts vertical highlight reels per presenter.

Thomas Au brought over eight years of professional editing experience to the opposite side of the tradeoff: why pure code-generated video is hard to fine-tune. While AI tools can assemble a full draft, they struggle with the rhythm and micro-timing of human editing. Thomas demonstrated a bridge connecting Claude Code and Remotion into CapCut and Adobe After Effects so editors keep a multi-track timeline and native keyframes without re-rendering whole sequences from scratch. He also demoed a web-based easing curve tool that visualizes motion presets like bounces, fast cuts, and swooshes along a time axis and converts them into copyable prompts for ChatGPT and ChatCut.

Khalil Nooh shared how he builds animated video drafts and UI motion components under strict budget and schedule constraints. Working within an existing $100 ChatGPT and Codex allocation rather than adding new subscriptions, he queries his personal knowledge vault to draft Kurzgesagt-style scripts for an upcoming book and renders draft animated clips with Codex, weighing Remotion against Hyperframe with input from peers. For interactive assessment cards (300-CORE Assessment), he generates HTML motion layouts on Claude's free tier and refines them in Codex. To break out of repetitive default color palettes, he directs the coding agent to pick palettes from Sanzo Wada's Dictionary of Color Combinations. He also noted calling voice agents like Grokbot during one-hour traffic commutes to keep ideation moving.

## Motion Transfer, Ad Replication, and Content Repurposing

Commercial ad generation and motion transfer drew sharp questions on unit economics and control.

Ken Ooi demonstrated AI motion transfer and face replacement using Power Studio's node-and-wire canvas for A47 alongside Higgsfield's GenJetsu model. Wiring character photos and 10-second cropped video clips into a visual graph, he swapped workspace attendees into a music video parody while keeping sunglasses intact. Ken highlighted why node canvases beat chat interfaces for media: you can see exactly which image context feeds each node. He also flagged clear limits: backgrounds frequently render inaccurately, spatial region annotation is not yet native to video models the way it is in some image models, the demonstrated run cost $33, and face-upload tools need strict consent safeguards. He closed by introducing Humio's VideoClaw B2B ad agent.

## Chat Agents, Podcast Prep, and In-Engine Game Directors

Rounding out the night were three workflows applying AI to daily messaging, podcast prep, and game engines.

James Yeang demonstrated an automated morning brief and personal assistant built on the Instinct platform inside WhatsApp. Connected to Google Calendar and email, the agent compiles daily schedules, priority tasks, and curated news digests under James's Morning Brief, complete with generated infographics (shown with an Android anti-scam story). Explicit like and dislike buttons train the curation over time, and users can ask follow-up questions about articles directly in chat. James pointed out that WhatsApp offers the lowest friction for non-technical users like parents or spouses, who rarely build custom workflows but gladly forward dense messages for quick summaries or ask about their schedule in chat.

Faris Irfan presented Host Mode, a multi-agent podcast preparation pipeline built for himself and co-host Hamza. Two Doom Scroller bots pull tech, startup, and AI updates from X and Instagram, relying on Faris's own interested and not interested feed signals as an ingestion filter. Topics are passed to Herald to outline episode flow and to Wisma to render a mobile web view with two to three concise talking points per topic instead of a rigid script. The pipeline also uses Grokbot to produce short video clips with headlines and music, though Faris noted early Instagram trial reels had modest reach and the auto-generated background music was too dull for episode two.

For MELAKA 1511, developed with Artifisi Sdn Bhd under the Digital Creators Programme, off-the-shelf generative video tools hallucinated heavily when trying to render their proprietary 3D voxel assets. Instead, the team used Claude as an in-engine Godot director: from a short plot outline, Claude scripts character paths, camera sweeps, lighting, and day-night transitions on their 20 percent complete build, capturing stills and captions for pitch trailers without hiring external animators. He also showed Dunia, a Godot and Hugging Face Space setup that records player telemetry alongside synchronized RGB frames, depth maps, and semantic segmentation for training multimodal AI models.

## Practical Takeaways from the Room

Across twelve very different setups, a few patterns stood out for anyone building with AI right now:

- **Plan before you render:** Whether paying $20 to $33 per video run or waiting 40 minutes for a local render, locking down brand guidelines, narrative beats, and storyboards upfront saves both time and compute.
- **Keep an escape hatch to editable tools:** Pure prompt iteration hits a wall on fine pacing and voice. Exposing keyframes in CapCut or After Effects, or treating LLM scripts as an 80 percent draft, keeps quality high.
- **Use existing engines when assets already exist:** For 3D worlds like MELAKA 1511, letting an LLM direct Godot beats text-to-video generation on consistency and cost.
- **Meet users where they already are:** Delivering morning briefs and summaries inside WhatsApp removes the onboarding barrier for non-technical users.
