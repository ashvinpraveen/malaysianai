Twelve builders gathered in Kuala Lumpur on 5 November 2026 for Malaysian AI's weekly Show & Tell. Hardware dominated the room, with multiple ESP32 and Arduino builds alongside smart glasses, on-device phone sensors, multi-machine coding setups, and domain-specific AI workflows.

## ESP32 hardware, robotics, and microcontroller builds

### Syukri Arabi: ESP Presenter

Physical laser pointers do not show up well on TV screens, and commercial tools like a roughly RM300 Logitech Spotlight require installing companion software on the host computer. Syukri built ESP Presenter to solve both problems using an Android phone and an ESP32-S3 board.

The Android APK sends commands over Bluetooth to the ESP32-S3, which acts as a USB keyboard for a Mac so nothing needs to be installed on the presentation machine. During the live demo, he used the phone's volume buttons to change slides, moved a circular spotlight and red laser dot across the TV, drew and cleared annotations, and triggered embedded device scripts to open Claude Code and Codex. He said he built the project with Codex in about two hours, spending most of that time fixing the mobile UI.

### Jun: ESP32 and LiDAR autonomous vehicle

Jun walked through his open-source GitHub documentation for a SLAM robot built with an ESP32-S3, an RPLIDAR A1, wheel encoders, a DRV8833 motor driver, motors, and a battery. In his architecture, the ESP32 collects sensor data while a PC handles mapping calculations and navigation using Nav2.

A practical lesson from his build was how much encoder timing matters: slow Wi-Fi and dropped encoder readings during rotation cause map overlap, which can warp a square room into a circle. Asked about beginner costs, he broke down the core parts at around RM300 (two encoders at RM25 each, a budget LiDAR around RM200, and an ESP32-S3 board at RM50), estimating a full build under RM500 before adding a camera.

### Andrew: Kubernetes on ESP32 for distributed neural networks and Doom

To move beyond single-microcontroller demos, Andrew described porting Kubernetes to three ESP32 boards. He walked through two deployments: a distributed neural-network setup across two nodes and five pods (keyword recognition, a diffusion model, and a small story-writing LLM) designed to migrate workloads to his Mac or another node if a device drops out, followed by multiplayer Doom across ESP32 boards with a second player joining from a browser. Room Wi-Fi interference made the live Doom run choppy, and the HDMI feed eventually dropped, a familiar reminder of live hardware demo constraints.

### Amara: Yes-or-no decision device

Amara brought a small wired Arduino prototype with a blue button that works like a Magic 8 Ball for solo-founder task paralysis, returning answers like "Very doubtful" or "Reply hazy, try again." She assembled it from an Arduino starter kit bought on Lazada for 70 using instructions from Claude. Beyond being a playful desk tool (with a necklace suggested as a possible future form factor during Q&A), it served as a test build for a female robotics workshop she is planning.

## Wearables, voice assistants, and on-device sensors

### Jack Min Ong: Even Realities Even G2 smart glasses

Jack brought a pair of Even Realities Even G2 smart glasses he bought primarily for live translation, noting that they detect language switches such as Chinese and Malay and also support news, stock graphs, calendars, to-do lists, and talk transcription. In Q&A, he explained that the glasses are a one-off purchase with currently free app and AI usage (though he noted that might not stay free forever) and prescription lenses take about three weeks. Comparing them with Meta's glasses, he preferred a dual-eye display over speaker-based translation because listening to both a speaker and audio translation at once makes it hard to catch up, though he felt Meta's wristband gesture controls were stronger.

### Nathan Hor: Portable business assistant and client dashboard

Nathan presented a portable voice assistant aimed at helping business users research clients before meetings, record conversations, and track follow-ups in a browser dashboard showing connections, conversations, follow-ups, speaker cards, and audio recordings. He described using it on a car dashboard while his phone runs navigation, and noted that voice lookups are still rough around the edges and speaker attribution can miss. During Q&A, he shared a practical privacy boundary: he keeps people search tied to public LinkedIn profiles first after an early Facebook search surfaced family members.

### Devansh Panchal: upright fall and accident detection

Devansh introduced upright, a phone app designed to detect falls and accidents using a small on-device sensor model trained with Claude on fall-detection datasets. He explained that the app samples phone sensors 50 times per second without needing internet access and waits 60 seconds after a fall before alerting a chosen friend rather than calling an ambulance directly. On screen, the live dashboard displayed g-force readings and a red "Are you OK?" countdown dialog. He also described filtering hospital data to exclude non-emergency clinics such as eye care and IVF centres.

## Multi-machine agents and owned external memory

### Mohtasham Murshid Madani: Distributed compute for coding agents

Mohtasham walked through his personal Tailscale network connecting a MacBook Air, a Google Cloud VM, a newly acquired office Mac Mini, a home Windows PC, and a serverless bot. Rather than running Kubernetes, he uses Tailscale so he can launch coding agent threads across three separate locations and still keep two nodes online if one site loses power. He is currently migrating workloads from his GCP VM to the Mac Mini, with services configured to restart on login.

### Khalil Nooh: Proto BMC and external-memory workspace

Following a Business Model Canvas masterclass, Khalil built Proto under TFIS for non-technical builders and knowledge workers who use multiple AI tools, including free ChatGPT accounts. Using Codex, he built MCP plugins with OAuth sign-in so ChatGPT can pull workspace context and submit structured canvas updates back to Proto for human review and approval. The goal is to keep accumulated project memory and judgment in an external system the user owns instead of locked inside one chat provider.

## Creative production and civic co-creation

### Lesly Leon Lee: AI-assisted event-video editing app

As an event videographer who routinely cuts 1.5 to 2 hours of footage into 30 to 60 second highlights, Lesly found plenty of tools for talking-head clips but very few for editing real event footage. He started building Event Prepass (now v2.3) on Windows with Claude and Gemini, using event presets and reference videos to guide shot tagging. He shared a roughly 20-second Chinese banquet cut generated in one pass, noting that because he used a boxing-event video as the reference, the pacing came out very fast, and mentioned linking the workflow to his video editor via a Claude connector for manual tweaks.

### Faris Irfan: Creative-design foundation model and marketing-studio site

Faris discussed his work on a vertical foundation model for creative design and showed a mobile website built for a marketing agency that previously had only PDFs, brand colours, and WhatsApp groups. He described how the tool structured portfolio sections and designed a brand-coloured play button while avoiding generic vibe-coded colour palettes, though the earlier version did not load for a side-by-side comparison and the client CRM stayed private. He recommended pairing design tools with ChatGPT and Claude, and offered free pilot access alongside pay-as-you-go pricing to gather more real projects.

### Frankie: Neighbourhood journey co-creation

Frankie walked through a civic design concept for residents and urban planners to map difficult everyday journeys together. His screens showed an illustrated neighbourhood map connecting homes, a bus stop, and a market hall, along with a "Review priorities" view and visualisations of proposed street improvements. He proposed feeding saved community input into a live dashboard so decision-makers can spot recurring neighbourhood complaints and test built-environment changes.
